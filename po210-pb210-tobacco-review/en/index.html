Polonium-210 and Lead-210 in Tobacco and Tobacco Smoke: A Review of Sources

Content in tobacco, uptake pathways into the plant, transfer to smoke, behaviour in the airways and the smoker’s radiation dose according to primary sources from 1964–2024. Abstract

The object of review is the natural radionuclides polonium-210 (210Po) and lead-210 (210Pb) in raw tobacco, cigarette smoke and the smoker’s body. The topic is relevant because estimates of smokers’ exposure published since 1964 differ by more than three orders of magnitude, and part of the difference arises not from the physics of the process but from the incomparability of the measured quantities and from differing calculation assumptions. The aim of this work is to consolidate, from primary sources, measurements of specific activity, data on uptake pathways into the plant, on transfer to smoke and on behaviour in the airways, and dose estimates, separating measured values from modelled ones and primary sources from paraphrases.

The review covers direct measurements of 210Po, 210Pb, 226Ra, 228Ra and 228Th in tobacco; activity balances between ash, butt, filter, mainstream and sidestream smoke; two versions of isotope uptake into the leaf — root and atmospheric; tissue concentrations; and local, mean and effective doses. A comparison is made with measurements of 210Pb and 210Po in herbaceous vegetation.

Methods: reading full texts where available and abstracts otherwise; comparison of sources without averaging discrepancies; checking every reference against the content of the primary source; recalculation of units, ratios and doses by an independent calculation.

Conclusion: the specific activity of 210Po in the cigarette tobacco of most brands studied is 12–25 Bq/kg (14–22 mBq per cigarette); values obtained in the 1960s and in the 2000s–2020s lie in the same interval, no consistent long-term trend has been established across studies, and values up to 40–60 Bq/kg refer to unprocessed raw tobacco and to particular markets. This interval lies within the range measured for herbaceous biomass, and a 210Pb/226Ra ratio of typically 2–4 shows that lead enters the leaf independently of soil radium. Mainstream smoke carries 3–36 % of the 210Po activity depending on the brand and smoking regime; values of 50–75 % refer to total smoke including the sidestream. Effective doses from 210Po and 210Pb for a pack-a-day smoker calculated with explicit assumptions, 0.06–0.8 mSv/year, differ mainly in the assumed inhaled fraction (0.07–0.75, up to 1.0 for some authors) and in dose coefficients, not in the activity of the tobacco. The local dose to the epithelium of bronchial bifurcations is a quantity of a different kind, and comparing it with the effective dose without qualification is incorrect.

Section 1 Introduction

The presence of 210Po in tobacco and its transfer to cigarette smoke were demonstrated experimentally by Radford and Hunt in January 1964 [1]. The response was immediate and varied: by May of the same year an editorial in Circulation listed polonium among smoke constituents “that may be significant in the genesis of lung cancer in heavy smokers” [37]; in December 1965 the first Soviet study with direct measurements appeared [4], and its editorial afterword refuted the figure of “25 rem per pack of cigarettes” popularised by a popular-science magazine — such a dose, the editors noted, accumulates only over several decades [4]; tobacco manufacturers, as internal industry documents later showed, had confirmed the published levels with their own measurements by 1968 [15].

Dozens of studies measuring the activity of 210Po and its parent 210Pb in raw tobacco, ash, filter, smoke and tissues have since been published, yet no consistent picture of the quantitative contribution of these isotopes to smokers’ exposure has emerged. A 2019 systematic review states that the data on radionuclide content of tobacco and smoke are robust, but that the field “has been scientifically stagnant for the last 40 years”, and that a subset of contemporary authors suggests individual risks about a thousand times higher than internationally accepted models support [29]. Part of the discrepancy is methodological: under the single heading of “transfer to smoke” different studies measure different quantities, and under the heading of “dose” either the local tissue dose, the mean organ dose or the effective dose.

This review is organised by questions rather than by sources: how much radionuclide tobacco contains (Section 2); where it enters the leaf from (Section 3) and how the 210Po/210Pb ratio changes with time (Section 4); what a comparison with herbaceous vegetation, for which an independent body of data exists, shows (Section 5); what part of the activity passes into smoke and into which stream (Section 6); what happens to the isotopes in the airways (Section 7); how dose estimates are derived from these data and why they differ (Section 8); and what is known about the industry’s position (Section 9). Discrepancies between sources are not averaged: values are given with their method and percentage basis.

Section 2 Radionuclides in tobacco

Polonium-210

The first measurements of the 1960s and modern studies give values in the same interval for cigarette tobacco. Ferri and Baratta (1966, U.S. Public Health Service) obtained for six cigarette types a mean of 0.43 pCi 210Po per gram of tobacco (15.9 Bq/kg) and 0.37 pCi per cigarette (13.7 mBq); the same paper cites the Radford and Hunt mean of 0.425 pCi per cigarette (15.7 mBq) with an 85 % material balance [28]. Ermolaeva-Makovskaya, Pertsov and Popov (1965) found 3.0–5.6 × 10−13 Ci per cigarette (11–21 mBq) for twelve domestic cigarette brands and 5.0–17.8 × 10−13 Ci (19–66 mBq) for nine foreign ones; the mean concentration in domestic cigarette tobacco was (4.3 ± 1.0) × 10−13 Ci/g, i.e. 15.9 Bq/kg [4]. According to Nikolova’s compilation (1970), US tobacco leaf in 1938–1963 contained on average 0.345 and 0.551 pCi/g 210Po (12.8 and 20.4 Bq/kg) in two measurement series; the same compilation gives, citing Ermolaeva-Makovskaya, a mean of 1.127 pCi/g (41.7 Bq/kg; 0.600 to 1.600 pCi/g) for raw tobacco of the USSR and 0.754 pCi/g (27.9 Bq/kg) for Rhodesia [14]. Philip Morris’s internal measurements gave 0.33–0.36 pCi/g (12–13 Bq/kg) by 1968 — a value matching the published one [15].

Modern studies: Berthet et al. (Lausanne, 2022) found for thirteen brands on the Swiss market 15.0 ± 2.3 mBq per cigarette, or 25.2 ± 2.6 mBq/g (n = 15; 12.7–21.2 mBq per cigarette by brand) [2]; Khater (2004) found for ten brands on the Egyptian market 16.3 ± 3.2 mBq per cigarette (21.0 ± 4.0 mBq/g; 9.7–22.5 mBq by brand) and, comparing the result with a 1968 measurement for the same Egyptian cigarettes (14.1 mBq), concluded that modern tobacco processing has not reduced the 210Po content [7]. Skwarzec et al. (2001) found up to 24.1 mBq per cigarette for the 14 best-selling brands on the Polish market [35]; Horváth et al. (2017) — 9.7 ± 1.2 to 26.5 ± 4.6 mBq per cigarette for 17 brands sold in Iran [3]; Cankurt and Görgün (2020) — 16.1 to 37.6 mBq, mean 22.4 ± 1.5 mBq, for 16 brands in Turkey [31]; Ghanbar-Moghaddam and Fathivand (2020) — a mean of 38.4 mBq/g for Iranian and 20.0 mBq/g for imported cigarettes [36]. Compilations: according to Khater and to Skwarzec et al., world data lie in the range 2.8–37 Bq/kg [7], [35]; Persson and Holm (2011) give a literature mean of 13 ± 2 Bq/kg and note that it is “rather constant over time and geographical origin” [5]; Beckman’s textbook gives 55 Bq/kg for tobacco without citing a source [27]; the review by Felix and Ntarisa (2024), covering 40 studies from 2000–2023, gives a 210Po mean of 20.4 mBq/g with a range of 0.4–128.6 mBq/g, 210Pb 15.4 (2.0–78.8) mBq/g and 226Ra 5.9 (2.0–16.0) mBq/g [30].

The spread between brands within a single study reaches a factor of several (for foreign cigarettes in Ermolaeva-Makovskaya et al., from 5.0 to 17.8 × 10−13 Ci, a factor of 3.6 [4]) and is comparable with the difference between the means of different decades: the means for cigarette tobacco of the 1960s (0.33–0.55 pCi/g, 12–20 Bq/kg) and of the 2000s–2020s (13–25 Bq/kg) lie in the same interval. Unprocessed raw tobacco lies above this interval: in Ermolaeva-Makovskaya et al. domestic raw tobacco grades contain 6.0–16.0 × 10−13 Ci/g (22–59 Bq/kg, mean 41.7 Bq/kg) [4], which Nikolova attributes to the decay of 210Po during fermentation and storage [14]; among market cigarettes, local Iranian brands (mean 38 Bq/kg) [36] and individual Turkish brands (up to 37.6 mBq per cigarette) [31]. Cankurt and Görgün report an increase in 210Po in cigarettes on the Turkish market over the last two decades [31], Persson and Holm, from a literature compilation, constancy over time [5]; across studies no consistent long-term trend has been established.

Other radionuclides

Ferri and Baratta measured 226Ra, 210Pb, 210Bi and 210Po in the same cigarettes: per cigarette, 226Ra 0.11–0.17 pCi (4.1–6.3 mBq), 210Pb 0.33–0.49 pCi (12.2–18.1 mBq; for three of the six types 210Pb was taken equal to the measured 210Bi), 210Po 0.32–0.48 pCi (11.8–17.8 mBq) [28]. Papastefanou (2009) measured by gamma spectrometry 17 samples of tobacco leaf from the 1990 harvest in 15 regions of Greece: 226Ra 1.80–7.57 Bq/kg (mean 3.38), 228Ra 1.10–6.52 (mean 3.83), 210Pb 6.34–18.2 (mean 14.12), 40K 273–2080 (mean 823), 137Cs 1.20–7.00 Bq/kg (mean 3.40) — against 70–180 Bq/kg 137Cs in 1986 leaf according to a tobacco factory certificate [11].

Petushkov, Zel’tser and Medvedovsky (1972) noted that the total alpha activity of tobacco exceeds that of polonium alone by a factor of 4–5, and determined by an emanation method 228Th (radiothorium) in four brands of cigarettes and papirosy: 0.36–0.55 pCi/g (13–20 Bq/kg), i.e. of the same order as 210Po; 0.09 pCi remained in the ash of a smoked cigarette [40]. More than forty years later Kubalek et al. (2016) measured uranium, thorium, radium, lead and polonium isotopes in Slovenian cigarettes and estimated that the contribution of thorium isotopes to the effective dose (6, 47 and 37 µSv/year from 228Th, 230Th and 232Th, 90 in total) exceeds that of the 210Po + 210Pb pair (61 + 9 µSv/year) [16]. The review by Felix and Ntarisa of 40 studies gives 232Th 8.1 (0.3–41.0) mBq/g and 238U 15.2 (0.2–82.0) mBq/g in tobacco, and a mean annual dose of 889.7 µSv from 232Th against 295 µSv from 210Po [30]. Papastefanou pointed out that the contribution of radium isotopes is usually disregarded in the literature, although it is of the same order as that of 210Pb [11]. Thus 210Po is the most studied, but not the only alpha emitter in tobacco.

Section 3 Uptake pathways into the plant: root or atmosphere

The pathway by which 210Po and 210Pb enter the tobacco leaf has been debated since the mid-1960s, and the first studies already contained both versions. Ermolaeva-Makovskaya et al. (1965) concluded that the 210Po in cigarettes is in equilibrium with 210Pb “released on the surface of tobacco leaves”, and that 210Pb “forms in the air from radon emanating from the soil” [4]. Berger, Erhardt and Francis (1965), on the basis of soil-extract analyses, reached the same conclusion in cautious form: there are indications that 210Po or its precursors are taken up not by the roots but by sorption in dead, moist plant material at the atmosphere–plant interface; 210Po could not be detected in green tobacco leaf, whereas cured leaf and soils contained it [10]. Tso, Harley and Alexander (1966), in an experiment with a chamber enriched in radon-222, in fields with different phosphate fertilisers and in culture with 210Pb in the nutrient solution, reached the opposite conclusion: the major portion of 210Pb in the plant was probably absorbed through the roots, while airborne radon and its daughters contributed much less; the final 210Po content was affected by the stage of leaf development and the curing method [9]. As reported by Ferri and Baratta, Marsden (1964) considered the contribution of atmospheric polonium fallout minor compared with root uptake [28]; Nikolova (1970), on the contrary, notes that the absence of a link between the alpha activity of grass and soil “emphasises the importance of the non-root pathway” [14].

Ferri and Baratta found 210Pb/226Ra activity ratios of 1.9–4.1 in cigarettes (4.3 in pipe tobacco) and concluded that 210Pb reaches tobacco by direct uptake from soil and air rather than merely as the daughter of radium in the tobacco itself [28]. Martell (1974) formulated the mechanism that later became generally accepted: airborne 210Pb is concentrated on small Aitken particles that accumulate on tobacco trichomes; curing and the combustion of trichomes produce insoluble particles of high 210Pb activity that the smoker inhales [13]. Papastefanou (2009) notes that about 85 % of tobacco leaf trichomes have glandular heads coated with sticky exudate and, on the basis of his own measurements, assigns nuclides to origins: 210Pb is deposited from the air and trapped by trichomes; radium isotopes enter through the roots from soil or with fertilisers; 40K through the roots; 137Cs and 134Cs through the roots, since four years after the Chernobyl accident they remained in the soil [11]. Persson and Holm (2011) distinguish polonium supported by uptake from soil from unsupported polonium deposited from the atmosphere; for leafy plants the 210Po level is particularly high owing to direct deposition [5]. Berthet et al. adopt the atmospheric version in their introduction [2]; Cankurt and Görgün (2020), who determined 210Po monthly in tobacco from the Aegean region of Turkey, proceed from the capture of aerosols by sticky trichomes on both leaf surfaces; they found in the leaf a mean of 14.1 ± 1.1 Bq/kg dry mass (from 8.3 in June to 21.7 in July), 7.6 in the stem, 14.3 in the root and 34.4 Bq/kg in the soil, a soil-to-leaf transfer factor of 0.22–0.65, and a negative relationship between 210Po in the leaf and rainfall [31]. Ghanbar-Moghaddam and Fathivand (2020) name drying of tobacco in direct contact with fossil-fuel flue gases as an additional source of 210Po [36].

The role of phosphate fertilisers is not generally accepted in the sources reviewed. According to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, the specific activity of 226Ra in phosphate fertilisers is approximately 0.185–1.11 Bq/g (5–30 pCi/g) depending on the blend; during processing of phosphate rock about 80 % of the 226Ra concentrates in phosphogypsum [12]. Papastefanou gives 238U 312–936 Bq/kg, 226Ra 19–1129 Bq/kg and 40K 53–6370 Bq/kg for phosphate fertilisers [11]. Zagà et al. (2011) list fertilisers among the main sources “according to several studies” and, citing a 1980 internal industry document, report that Martell’s recommendation to replace calcium phosphate fertilisers with ammonium phosphate was known to manufacturers and regarded as “an expensive point” [8]. The brief review by Zagà, Cattaruzza and Gallus (2024) arranges the pathways in a hierarchy: principal — deposition of radon-222 decay products on the leaf with capture by trichomes; a smaller share — root uptake; additional — fertilisers from raw material rich in uranium [38].

Section 4 The 210Po/210Pb ratio and storage time of the leaf

The half-life of 210Po (138.4 d) is short compared with that of 210Pb (22.2 years), so after harvest the 210Pb activity is practically constant and the activity ratio relaxes to unity according to R(t) = 1 + (R0 − 1)·e−λt, where R0 is the ratio at harvest and λ is the decay constant of 210Po. For R0 = 0.32 this formula gives 0.72, 0.89 and 0.98 after 180, 365 and 730 d; for R0 = 1.54, 1.22, 1.09 and 1.01.

Measurements on tobacco agree. Ferri and Baratta described the effect directly: in pipe tobacco 210Pb and 210Po were not in equilibrium (0.41 and 0.20 pCi/g, a ratio of 0.49; 210Pb taken equal to the measured 210Bi), which the authors attributed to the tobacco being sold before equilibrium was established, whereas ageing of cigarette tobacco for about two years allows 210Pb and its daughters 210Bi and 210Po to reach equilibrium; in the cigarettes in which all three isotopes were measured (three types of six), their activities agreed within the uncertainty [28]. Khater states that more than two years usually pass between harvest and cigarette production — by his estimate 6–7 half-lives of 210Po (two years correspond to 5.3 half-lives) — and polonium approaches secular equilibrium with lead [7]; Berthet et al. measured a ratio of 1.06 ± 0.05 in cigarettes on the Swiss market [2]. Nikolova, by contrast, explained the lower 210Po content in finished products compared with leaf by decay of polonium during fermentation and warehouse storage [14] — possible only for leaf in which polonium exceeded lead at harvest (R0 > 1), as in Slovenian silage (Section 5).

Practical consequence: the 210Po activity in smoked tobacco is determined by the activity of 210Pb accumulated by the leaf during growth, not by the polonium content at harvest. It also follows that 210Pb matters for the smoker’s exposure no less than polonium: it determines both the 210Po activity in the cigarette and — through insoluble smoke particles — the ingrowth of polonium in the tissues themselves (Section 7).

Section 5 Comparison with data for herbaceous vegetation

Tobacco leaf is the above-ground leafy biomass of an annual plant. For such biomass (forage, hay, silage, wild medicinal herbs) a body of 210Pb and 210Po measurements exists that was obtained independently of the smoking problem. The comparison is made on five criteria: specific activity level, 210Po/210Pb ratio, uptake pathway, the role of soil radium, and the strength of binding of deposited lead. The conclusions of this section are the interpretation of the present review, not statements of the cited works.

The specific activity of 210Po in cigarette tobacco (12–25 Bq/kg) lies within the wide range for herbaceous biomass — from fractions of a becquerel to hundreds of becquerels per kilogram — and is close to the values for Brazilian forage and Slovenian hay (14–26 Bq/kg 210Pb); for maize silage and alfalfa grown on phosphatic clay soils (Staples et al., 1994) the compilation [24] gives much lower 210Pb values, 0.52–1.04 Bq/kg dry mass. The most direct comparison is provided by tobacco leaf measured before fermentation and cigarette production: 210Pb in Greek leaf averages 14.1 Bq/kg [11] and 210Po in growing Turkish leaf 14.1 ± 1.1 Bq/kg [31] — practically the same value as 210Pb in Slovenian hay and silage (14.4 and 14.7 Bq/kg [19]). No accumulation mechanism specific to tobacco is needed to explain this level.

The 210Po/210Pb ratio and time after harvest

In freshly harvested biomass 210Po and 210Pb are not in equilibrium: the activity ratio is 0.32 in Slovenian hay and 1.54 in silage [19]. By the formula of Section 4 both values reach 0.98–1.01 after two years of storage — the same ratio as measured in cigarettes (1.06 ± 0.05 [2]) and predicted by the roughly two-year ageing of cigarette tobacco [7], [28]. The disequilibrium in pipe tobacco (0.49 relative to 210Bi [28]) corresponds by the same formula to storage of about two months at R0 ≈ 0.3, typical of hay.

Uptake pathway

For herbaceous vegetation the sources indicate a predominance of the atmospheric pathway. According to Beckman, the main pathway of vegetation contamination with 210Pb is deposition on above-ground parts with aerosols (about 85 %), the root pathway about 15 %, and the 210Pb content of grass is determined by the amount of precipitation rather than by the soil content [27]. According to Bakhur et al., root uptake accounts for up to 20 % of 210Po and up to 14 % of 210Pb; 210Pb accumulates mainly in the upper, young above-ground parts, and the roots of bilberry and marsh tea contain 2–3 times less polonium and 3–4 times less lead than the above-ground part [26]. Pietrzak-Fliś and Skowrońska-Smolak (1995), in an experiment comparing an open field with a polyethylene tent, found that in the above-ground parts of plants on the open field the 210Pb and 210Po activities were higher than under the tent, whereas in the roots they were nearly the same; the authors regard transfer via the root system as negligible and name atmospheric, mainly wet, deposition as the main source [23]; according to Persson’s compilation (2013), the deposition transfer factor for grass and leafy plants is 0.5–1 (Bq/kg plant per Bq/m² deposited), 0.1–0.5 for less leafy plants and straw, below 0.2 and below 0.003 for grain and root crops, and about three times higher for 210Po than for 210Pb [24]. Jovanovič (2011), on the tailings of the former Žirovski Vrh uranium mine, found that the measured 210Pb activity in grass was up to a hundred times higher than that calculated from the soil content, and attributed this to deposition of radon-222 decay products from the air [22].

Tobacco leaf is broad and densely covered with glandular trichomes [11], so by its surface properties it presumably intercepts airborne particles no less effectively than grass. By analogy, the data for herbaceous vegetation support the atmospheric version [2], [4], [5], [10], [13] and are inconsistent with the conclusion of Tso et al. [9]. The analogy does not, however, refute the result of a controlled experiment: no paired open-field versus shelter measurements on tobacco were found in the sources reviewed, and the radon-chamber experiment of Tso et al. is the only controlled experiment on tobacco itself.

Soil radium and fertilisers

According to the IAEA handbook (Technical Reports Series No. 472), the mean soil-to-plant transfer factor for stems and shoots of grasses (category Grasses) in temperate climates is 0.31 for lead (N = 17) and 0.13 for radium (N = 62), and for pasture vegetation 0.092 for lead, 0.12 for polonium (N = 10) and 0.071 for radium [25]. The soil-to-leaf factor for 210Po in tobacco, 0.22–0.65 [31], is of the same order. With such values root uptake from soil radium is limited, and raising the soil radium content through fertilisers presumably affects 210Pb and 210Po in the leaf mainly through radon emanation from the soil and deposition of its decay products on the leaf. The tailings case [22] shows this mechanism in its extreme form: the radium of the substrate affects 210Pb in grass through the air, not through the root.

In paired measurements of forage and hay the 210Pb/226Ra ratio is 3.2 [18], 11.5 and 24 [19] (per [24]), whereas in the soil under the Brazilian forage 210Pb and 226Ra are close (104 and 135 Bq/kg [24]), i.e. 210Pb in above-ground biomass greatly exceeds the level supported by the radium of the sample itself. In cigarettes this ratio is 1.9–4.1, in pipe tobacco 4.3 [28], and in Greek tobacco leaf before production 4.2 on average (14.1 and 3.4 Bq/kg) [11]: the same sign of disequilibrium and the same order as in forage. It follows that 210Pb in tobacco cannot be estimated from the 226Ra content of the leaf or soil; this is consistent with Papastefanou’s conclusion that radium in tobacco leaf is of root origin [11].

Strength of binding of deposited lead

According to Persson’s compilation (2013), after thorough washing 82 ± 20 % of atmospherically deposited 210Pb and 60 ± 20 % of root-derived 210Pb remain in the plant [24]. Tobacco data agree with this from an unexpected direction: according to Philip Morris internal documents of the mid-1970s, washing the leaf with non-polar solvents reduced its radioactivity by 10–40 %, and according to the testimony of a former company scientist about half could be washed off — “the other half was inside the tobacco” [15]. Lead deposited on the leaf is not an easily removable surface contamination either in grass or in tobacco.

Section 6 Transfer to cigarette smoke

Three quantities under one heading

Under the common heading of “transfer to smoke” the literature gives different quantities: the activity fraction in the mainstream (inhaled) smoke; the fraction in total smoke, including the sidestream released into the air between puffs; and the fraction not remaining in the ash, butt and filter — which depends on whether the cigarette is smoked down to the filter. In addition, the percentage is calculated sometimes relative to the activity of the whole cigarette and sometimes relative to the activity of the smoke. Mainstream. Ferri and Baratta, on an apparatus of their own design checked against the cigarette residues of three smokers, obtained 11.0 to 30.7 % of the activity of the whole cigarette in inhaled smoke depending on the brand (up to 35.7 % in the paper’s summary), with a material balance of 93–104 % [28]. Berthet et al., on a smoking machine in an intense regime, obtained 13.6 ± 4.2 % for 210Po (n = 24; 9.4–15.6 % for the three brands listed in the paper’s table) and 7 % for 210Pb [2]; Horváth et al. obtained 15 ± 10 % with a brand-to-brand spread of 3–36 % and pointed out that standard smoking machines are not suited to quantitative trapping of 210Po [3]. Kubalek et al., collecting smoke, exhaled air, ash and butts from volunteers, found that about 22 % of the cigarette’s 210Po is retained in the smoker’s body [16]. In the introduction to Khater’s paper, citing Radford and Hunt (1964) and Mussalo-Rauhamaa and Jaakkola (1985), it is stated that 6.5–22 % of the cigarette’s 210Po is found in mainstream smoke, 3.7 to 58 % according to other authors, and that according to Parfenov (1974) on average about 50 % of 210Po passes into smoke, 35 % remains in the butt and about 15 % in the ash [7]; Zagà et al. reproduce the same ranges [8]. Kelley (1965), smoking eleven brands in a standardised manner, found that the 210Po content of mainstream smoke was related not to the presence or design of a filter but to the amount of particulate matter in the smoke [34].

Total smoke. The “50 % to smoke” figure going back to Radford and Hunt and reproduced by Rajewsky and Stahlhofen and by Parfenov [7], [14] refers, as Table 3 shows, to the sum of mainstream and sidestream with the butt counted separately: in Ferri and Baratta the sum of the streams is 47–63 %, in Berthet et al. about 62 % (sidestream by difference). Khater’s 74.7 % was obtained by difference between the activity of tobacco with paper and that of ash with filter after volunteers smoked the cigarettes, and includes everything not left in the ash and filter; the author notes that the result depends on smoking habits and on the activity of the non-inhaled sidestream [7]. The apparent spread of “from a few per cent to 75 %” found in the review literature arises largely from mixing these bases.

Ermolaeva-Makovskaya et al. concluded that filters “practically do not retain” 210Po, and that only about 20 % of the polonium sorbed by the filter is associated with tarry substances [4]; Khater assessed the filter efficiency as “quite low” [7]. Nikolova noted that the lower 210Po content of inhaled smoke from filtered cigarettes does not yet imply a significant protective effect [14]. The industry’s internal research gave 40–50 % removal of polonium from smoke by a cellulose acetate filter, but not of “appreciable quantities” of the insoluble fraction; ion-exchange and copper-containing filters proved ineffective [15].

Volatility and disequilibrium in smoke

Berthet et al., heating tobacco uniformly over 50–600 °C, found that half of the 210Po is lost already at 255.9 ± 2.2 °C, whereas for 210Pb the same fraction is lost at 531 ± 17 °C; at 300 °C about 80 % of the polonium is released [2]. The difference in volatility, in the authors’ assessment, may partly explain why 13.6 % of 210Po but only 7 % of 210Pb pass into mainstream smoke, and why the isotopes are not in equilibrium in the smoke itself: the lead-supported fraction of polonium in mainstream smoke averaged 52 ± 16 % with a spread of 38–83 % between runs [2]. Ermolaeva-Makovskaya et al., extracting filters with ether, established that 210Po is “practically not associated with the products of dry distillation of tobacco” [4]; Kelley related it to the mass of particulate matter [34]; Martell and Radford — to insoluble particles from sintered trichome tips, enriched in 210Pb, from which polonium is stripped on combustion [13], [32].

Heated tobacco

For heated tobacco (IQOS Heets, a separate product) Berthet et al. obtained a mainstream transfer of 1.8 ± 0.3 % of 210Po and about 1.5 % of 210Pb, with a 210Po content of 23.7 ± 2.1 mBq/g in the stick — the same as in cigarettes; 78 % of the activity remained in the unconsumed tobacco [2]. The difference is explained not by specific countermeasures of the manufacturer but by the fact that only about 15 % of the tobacco in the stick is heated to the target temperature of 330 °C; uniform heating to 300 °C would release about 80 % of the 210Po [2], [6], [38]. The authors caution that the lower dose does not mean lower danger, since the calculation does not take the smoker’s habits or possible increased consumption into account [6].

Section 7 In the airways and tissues

The cigarette smoke aerosol has a particle diameter of about 300 nm [17]; according to measurements by Desorgher et al., 92 % of mainstream 210Po is retained by a 0.8 µm filter, and the median of the size distribution adopted in their model is 0.43 µm [6]. In both radon-exposed miners and smokers, 75 % of lung tumours are found in the branching airways of the bronchial tree and 15 % in the gas exchange region [17]. Deposition in the bronchial region, according to the calculation of Desorgher et al., is about 13 % of the inhaled activity — noticeably more than in the standard ICRP models [6].

Little, Radford, McCombs and Hunt (1965) measured 210Po in the lung tissues of smokers and non-smokers; according to later accounts, the highest local concentrations were found in the bronchial epithelium of segmental bifurcations, “the pulmonary sites where bronchogenic carcinomas frequently arise” [15], [33]. Desorgher et al. note that in sixty years these remain the only measurements of the localisation of 210Po in the bronchi of deceased smokers, and that in the absence of such data dosimetry has to rely on general models [6]. Martell (1974) and Radford and Martell (1975) linked local accumulation to insoluble particles enriched in 210Pb: polonium is stripped from them on combustion and grows in again after inhalation, so the 210Po/210Pb ratio in the particles serves as a clock; preliminary data on three smokers and two non-smokers gave a residence time of the particles in bronchial epithelium of 3–5 months, an estimate the authors consider likely to be low [13], [32].

Mean organ concentrations are given in Table 5. In Ferri and Baratta, 210Po in the lung parenchyma of smokers is three times that of the non-smoker, 2.8 times in the pulmonary lymph nodes and 2.4 times in the liver, with small samples that, in the authors’ words, can show only a trend [28]. The UNSCEAR 2000 report states that the 210Po concentration in the lung parenchyma of smokers is about three times that of non-smokers [17]; according to internal industry documents, mean tissue concentrations in smokers are “more than double” [15]. Petushkov et al. measured the thoron concentration in exhaled air: 0.28 ± 0.03 pCi/L in smokers against 0.14 ± 0.02 pCi/L in non-smokers (10 persons each), attributing the difference to 228Th in tobacco [40]. For comparison of levels: Antonova (1971), for residents of Leningrad without regard to smoking, obtained 210Po of 147 ± 6 in hair, 46 ± 3 in bone tissue and 23 ± 2 pCi/kg in liver (5.4; 1.7; 0.85 Bq/kg) [39]. Smoking is not the only intake route for 210Po: according to data cited by Zagà et al. from Spencer (1977), for an adult male smoker 77.3 % of daily 210Po intake comes from food and 17.4 % from cigarette smoke [8].

Section 8 Radiation dose to the smoker

Dose estimates in the literature refer to three different quantities: the local tissue dose at deposition sites, the mean lung dose and the effective dose. They differ by orders of magnitude and cannot be compared without qualification.

Local doses to the bronchial epithelium

The first estimate belongs to Radford and Hunt: according to the abstract, for a person smoking two packs a day the dose to the bronchial epithelium “probably is at least seven times” that from background sources, and in localised areas may reach 1000 rem or more in 25 years [1]. The abstract gives no value for the diffuse dose, and accounts differ: Nikolova gives about 36 rem in 25 years [14], Petushkov et al. 200 rem in 25 years [40]; the latter value coincides with the estimate of Little and Radford (1967) for the epithelium of lower-lobe bifurcations — 2 Sv over 25 years of smoking, cited by Zagà et al. [8]. Other estimates of the same class collected by Nikolova and Zagà: 1 rem in 25 years according to Rajewsky and 1 rem per year according to Hill (1965) [14]; 80 mSv/year to the epithelium at bifurcations for one and a half packs a day according to Winters and DiFranza (1982) [11]; about 16 Sv (80 rad) of cumulative alpha dose at the bifurcations of smokers who died of lung cancer according to Martell [8]. Zagà et al. (2011) cite from Radford and Hunt only the maximum value — up to 0.4 Sv a year or 10 Sv over 25 years — without indicating its local character [8].

Mean lung dose

Ferri and Baratta calculated the mean dose to the lungs as a whole (mass 1200 g, all inhaled material retained, effective half-time 18 d) for two packs a day: 33–92 mrem/year (0.33–0.92 mSv/year) depending on the brand; for the brand containing pipe tobacco the paper’s table gives 92 and the text 95 mrem/year [28]. The authors state that, allowing for the fraction of aerosol deposited in and cleared from the lungs, the values could be high by as much as a factor of eight, and that non-homogeneous deposition may produce local doses much greater than the mean [28].

The data of Table 6 show that the more than tenfold spread of effective doses (0.06–0.8 mSv/year) arises mainly not from the activity of the tobacco, which is of the same order for most authors (10–38 mBq per cigarette), but from three assumptions. The first is the inhaled fraction: Khater assumed 74.7 % in smoke and 50 % inhalation, i.e. 0.37 of the cigarette activity; Papastefanou 0.75; Desorgher et al. the measured 0.136 for polonium and 0.07 for lead. Desorgher et al. explicitly note that some authors use a transfer factor of 0.7–1.0, whereas measurements give less than 0.15, and that estimates in the literature range from 30 µSv to tens of mSv per year [6]; the upper part of this range lies outside Table 6. The second is the dose coefficient: for 210Pb it differs by a factor of about 27 (1.1 µSv/Bq in Papastefanou against 29.2 in Desorgher), depending on the absorption type assumed and on whether the enhanced deposition in the bronchial region is taken into account. The third is the absorption regime: moving from slow to moderate absorption, one and the same model gives 0.317 and 0.072 mSv/year [6]. Laking’s systematic review (2019) puts it the same way: the results remain sensitive to pharmacological assumptions about absorption and redistribution of inhaled radionuclides and to radiobiological assumptions, and a subset of contemporary authors still suggests individual health risks about 1000 times higher than internationally accepted models support [29].

The UNSCEAR 2000 report estimates the annual effective dose from uranium- and thorium-series radionuclides in body tissues at 120 µSv, the main contributor being 210Po; the value refers to the general population, no smoker-specific dose estimate is given in Volume I, but it is noted that tobacco “greatly increases” the intake of 210Pb and 210Po by smokers [17]. The same report states that the combined effect of smoking and radon on lung cancer risk is the only case for which epidemiological data clearly call for allowance for a more-than-additive effect [17].

A local dose of 10 Sv over 25 years (400 mSv/year) exceeds the effective doses of Table 6 for conventional cigarettes by a factor of 500–6800; this compares unlike quantities — a tissue dose at the deposition site and a dose averaged over organs with weighting factors. Ferri and Baratta made this distinction explicitly in 1966: their calculation refers “to the average dose over the entire lung tissue”, whereas non-homogeneous deposition gives local doses “much greater” [28].

Risk and share of cases

Attribution of “1–2 % of lung cancer cases”

The statement that 210Po is responsible for 2 % of smoking-induced lung cancer cases appears in popular accounts without reference to a specific study. The nearest published estimate of this kind is Radford’s letter to the New England Journal of Medicine (1982), according to which 210Po is responsible for about 1 % of lung cancers in the United States; Muggli et al., citing this estimate, convert it into more than 1600 deaths a year in the United States and 11 700 worldwide [15]. No peer-reviewed study substantiating the 2 % figure by calculation was found in preparing this review. Zagà et al. estimate the risk differently — 4 × 10−4 cases per year per smoker, i.e. 4 cases per 10 000 smokers per year, noting that the estimate does not account for the co-carcinogenic action [8]. The comparison with “300 chest X-rays a year” for one and a half packs a day, going back to Winters and DiFranza (1982) [15], depends on the dose assumed per radiograph (0.034 mSv in Zagà et al. [8]) and should not be reproduced without stating that basis.

Section 9 The tobacco industry’s position

Criticism of Radford and Hunt’s original estimates in the peer-reviewed literature consists mainly of methodological work on the transfer of the isotope to smoke (Section 6) and on dosimetric assumptions (Section 8), rather than of a refutation of the hypothesis of its contribution to exposure. Muggli et al. (2008), using about 1500 internal industry documents, showed that manufacturers learned of the presence of polonium in tobacco more than 40 years before publication, confirmed the published levels with their own measurements, attempted to remove it — by washing the leaf (10–40 %), selecting raw material, filters and genetic engineering of the plant — and failed; Philip Morris’s 210Po monitoring laboratory, operating from 1981–1982, was closed around 1986 because of the risk of “producing evidence that could compromise the company in litigation” [15]. The article’s title — “Waking a Sleeping Giant” — reproduces the wording of a 1978 internal note on the undesirability of publishing the company’s own results “for fear of waking a sleeping giant” [8], [15]. The authors propose a radiation-exposure warning on cigarette packs [15].

Section 10 Limitations of the review

The works of Radford and Hunt [1], Tso et al. [9], Berger et al. [10], Kelley [34], Martell [13], Radford and Martell [32] and Laking [29] are cited from their abstracts; the work of Little et al. [33] from the accounts in [6], [15]; the work of Horváth et al. [3] from its abstract and the publisher’s highlights; the works of Persson and Holm [5], Kubalek et al. [16], Skwarzec et al. [35], Felix and Ntarisa [30], Cankurt and Görgün [31] and Ghanbar-Moghaddam and Fathivand [36] from their abstracts. Internal industry documents, Radford’s 1982 estimate, and the works of Winters and DiFranza, Spencer, Little and Radford (1967), Holtzman and Ilcewicz, Parfenov, Rajewsky and Stahlhofen, and Hill are cited from the accounts in [7], [8], [11], [14], [15].

Data for herbaceous vegetation (Section 5) are cited for works [18] and [19] from the tables of the compilation [24], and for works [20]–[23] from abstracts; the review article [26] and the textbook [27] from their text without checking their primary sources. The mass basis of tobacco filler and of dried biomass is not identical; the comparison in Section 5 is valid to order of magnitude, but not at the level of individual percentages. In Table 6 assumptions are given only where they are visible in the text read; for works read from abstracts the assumption columns are empty.

Section 11 Conclusion

The presence of 210Po and 210Pb in tobacco and the transfer of part of their activity to smoke have been confirmed by independent measurements over sixty years in different countries. The mean specific activities of 210Po in cigarette tobacco obtained in the 1960s (12–20 Bq/kg) and in the 2000s–2020s (13–25 Bq/kg) lie in the same interval; higher values, up to 40–60 Bq/kg, refer to unprocessed raw tobacco and to particular markets, and no consistent long-term trend has been established across studies. This interval lies within the range measured for herbaceous biomass; the 210Pb/226Ra ratio in tobacco, typically 2–4, matches that of forage in sign and order and shows that lead enters the leaf independently of soil radium; the 210Po/210Pb ratio, close to unity in cigarettes and below unity in pipe tobacco, is consistent with ingrowth of polonium during storage of the leaf. Alongside polonium, tobacco contains 228Th, 226Ra and 228Ra at activities of the same order, whose dose contribution is rarely taken into account.

The pathway of isotope uptake into the leaf remains a matter of disagreement between sources: the only controlled experiment on tobacco (1966) pointed to the roots, whereas measurements on tobacco and on herbaceous vegetation, where atmospheric uptake of 210Pb predominates even on a substrate with high radium content, support the version of deposition of radon decay products on trichomes. Mainstream smoke carries 3–36 % of the 210Po activity depending on the brand and smoking regime; values around 50 % refer to the sum of mainstream and sidestream, up to 75 % to everything not left in the ash and filter. Polonium is more volatile than lead, so the isotopes are not in equilibrium in smoke, and in the bronchi polonium grows in again from insoluble particles enriched in 210Pb.

Effective doses from 210Po and 210Pb for a pack-a-day smoker calculated with explicit assumptions, 0.06–0.8 mSv/year, differ mainly in the assumed inhaled fraction (from the measured 0.07–0.14 to the assumed 0.75, up to 1.0 for some authors) and in dose coefficients, not in the activity of the tobacco; the local dose to the epithelium of bronchial bifurcations is a quantity of a different kind, and comparing the two without qualification is misleading. The figure of “2 % of lung cancer cases” has no verified link to a peer-reviewed calculation; the nearest published estimate is about 1 %, from Radford’s 1982 letter.

Section 12 Statement on the use of artificial intelligence

The review was prepared by an agentic session based on the Claude language model (Anthropic) under the direction of the operator. Role of the model: decomposing the question into independent search directions; collecting sources with background agents assigned to separate subject areas; in edition 3, re-reading all sources with four independent agents from full texts (where available), producing line-by-line extracts of figures and quotations; consolidating the findings; typesetting. Before publication the text underwent a self-audit: independent passes by checking agents with different scopes (bibliography and reference matching; numerical content against the primary sources) and an independent recalculation of units, ratios and doses; the discrepancies found were corrected. Role of the operator: setting the task, requiring priority for highly cited sources, deciding on the review structure, instructing that tobacco be compared with data for herbaceous vegetation, providing the full texts of works [4], [7], [28], [37]–[40] and the author’s manuscript of work [11] (the text uses the figures of the published article; the manuscript differs from it in the 228Ra maximum — 6.62 against 6.52 Bq/kg — and in lacking 40K data), pointing to related publications in PubMed, instructing that all sources be re-read, and deciding on publication. Citation counts of key works were obtained from the Crossref database on 25 September 2026.
