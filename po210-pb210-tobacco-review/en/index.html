Abstract

The object of review is the natural radionuclides polonium-210 (210Po) and lead-210 (210Pb) in raw tobacco, cigarette smoke and the smoker’s body. The topic is relevant because estimates of smokers’ exposure published since 1964 differ by more than three orders of magnitude, and part of the difference is due to the incomparability of the measured quantities and to differing calculation assumptions. The aim of this work is to systematise, from primary sources, measurements of specific activity, data on uptake pathways into the plant, on transfer to smoke and on behaviour in the airways, and dose estimates, distinguishing measured from calculated quantities and primary from secondary sources.

The review covers direct measurements of 210Po, 210Pb, 226Ra, 228Ra and 228Th in tobacco; activity balances between ash, butt, filter, mainstream and sidestream smoke; two mechanisms of isotope uptake into the leaf — root uptake and atmospheric deposition; tissue concentrations; and local, mean and effective doses. A comparison is made with measurements of 210Pb and 210Po in herbaceous vegetation.

Methods: analysis of the full texts of the works (where available) or of their abstracts; comparison of sources without averaging discrepancies; checking every reference against the content of the primary source; independent recalculation of units, activity ratios and doses.

Conclusion: the specific activity of 210Po in the cigarette tobacco of most brands studied is 12–25 Bq/kg (14–22 mBq per cigarette); values obtained in the 1960s and in the 2000s–2020s lie in the same interval, no consistent long-term trend has been established across studies, and values up to 40–60 Bq/kg refer to unprocessed raw tobacco and to particular markets. This interval lies within the range measured for herbaceous biomass, and a 210Pb/226Ra ratio of typically 2–4 shows that lead enters the leaf independently of soil radium. Mainstream smoke carries 3–36 % of the 210Po activity depending on the brand and smoking regime; values of 50–75 % refer to total smoke including the sidestream. Effective doses from 210Po and 210Pb for a pack-a-day smoker calculated with explicit assumptions, 0.06–0.8 mSv/year, differ mainly in the assumed inhaled fraction (0.07–0.75, up to 1.0 for some authors) and in dose coefficients, not in the activity of the tobacco. The local absorbed dose to the epithelium of bronchial bifurcations and the effective dose are quantities of different nature and are not directly comparable. By three independent methods of estimation, the contribution of 210Po and 210Pb to smoking-related mortality is 0.03–4 %, with a central value of the order of 0.2–0.3 %; the increase in a smoker’s absolute risk from indoor radon at 100 Bq/m³ is about 15 times this central estimate. The contribution of phosphate fertilisers has not been established: two controlled experiments of 1966–1968 reached opposite conclusions, and the radium input with fertiliser and the annual atmospheric deposition of 210Pb at the application rates used for tobacco are of the same order.

Section 1 Introduction

The presence of 210Po in tobacco and its transfer to cigarette smoke were demonstrated experimentally by Radford and Hunt in January 1964 [1]. The finding was taken up in several fields: in May of the same year an editorial in Circulation mentioned polonium among smoke constituents reported to be potentially significant in the pathogenesis of lung cancer in heavy smokers [37]; in December 1965 the first Soviet study with direct measurements was published [4], and its editorial afterword refuted the value of 25 rem per pack of cigarettes given in a popular-science publication — according to the editors, a dose of that order accumulates over several decades of smoking [4]; tobacco manufacturers, according to internal industry documents, had confirmed the published levels with their own measurements by 1968 [15].

Dozens of studies with measurements of the activity of 210Po and its parent 210Pb in raw tobacco, ash, filter, smoke and tissues have since been published, yet a consistent quantitative estimate of the contribution of these isotopes to smokers’ exposure is lacking. A 2019 systematic review states that the data on the radionuclide content of tobacco and smoke are robust, whereas research on the subject has made no scientific progress over the last forty years, and that some contemporary authors report individual risks about a thousand times higher than internationally accepted models support [29]. Part of the discrepancy is methodological: the term “transfer to smoke” denotes different measured quantities in different studies, and the term “dose” denotes the local tissue dose, the mean organ dose or the effective dose.

The material is arranged by subject: the specific activity of radionuclides in tobacco (Section 2); the pathways of their uptake by the plant (Section 3) and the change of the 210Po/210Pb ratio with time (Section 4); a comparison with data for herbaceous vegetation (Section 5); transfer to the mainstream and sidestream smoke (Section 6); the behaviour of the isotopes in the airways and tissues (Section 7); dose estimates and the reasons for their divergence (Section 8); the contribution of the radiation to the smoker’s risk and its relation to indoor radon exposure (Section 9); the position of the tobacco industry (Section 10). Discrepancies between sources are not averaged; values are given with their method and basis.

The spread between brands within a single study reaches a factor of 3–4 (for foreign cigarettes in Ermolaeva-Makovskaya et al., from 5.0 to 17.8 × 10−13 Ci, a factor of 3.6 [4]) and is comparable with the difference between the means of different decades: the means for cigarette tobacco of the 1960s (0.33–0.55 pCi/g, 12–20 Bq/kg) and of the 2000s–2020s (13–25 Bq/kg) lie in the same interval. Values above this interval refer to unfermented raw tobacco: in Ermolaeva-Makovskaya et al. domestic raw tobacco grades contain 6.0–16.0 × 10−13 Ci/g (22–59 Bq/kg, mean 41.7 Bq/kg) [4], which, according to Nikolova, may be explained by the decay of 210Po during fermentation and storage [14]; among cigarettes on the market, local Iranian brands (mean 38 Bq/kg) [36] and individual Turkish brands (up to 37.6 mBq per cigarette) [31]. Cankurt and Görgün report an increase in 210Po in cigarettes on the Turkish market over the last two decades [31], Persson and Holm, from a literature compilation, constancy over time [5]; across studies no consistent long-term trend has been established.

Other radionuclides

Ferri and Baratta measured 226Ra, 210Pb, 210Bi and 210Po in the same cigarettes: per cigarette, 226Ra 0.11–0.17 pCi (4.1–6.3 mBq), 210Pb 0.33–0.49 pCi (12.2–18.1 mBq; for three of the six types 210Pb was taken equal to the measured 210Bi), 210Po 0.32–0.48 pCi (11.8–17.8 mBq) [28]. Papastefanou (2009) measured by gamma spectrometry 17 samples of tobacco leaf from the 1990 harvest in 15 regions of Greece: 226Ra 1.80–7.57 Bq/kg (mean 3.38), 228Ra 1.10–6.52 (mean 3.83), 210Pb 6.34–18.2 (mean 14.12), 40K 273–2080 (mean 823), 137Cs 1.20–7.00 Bq/kg (mean 3.40) — against 70–180 Bq/kg 137Cs in 1986 leaf according to a tobacco factory certificate [11].

Petushkov, Zel’tser and Medvedovsky (1972) established that the total alpha activity of tobacco exceeds that of polonium alone by a factor of 4–5, and determined by an emanation method 228Th (radiothorium) in four brands of cigarettes and papirosy: 0.36–0.55 pCi/g (13–20 Bq/kg), i.e. of the same order as 210Po; 0.09 pCi remained in the ash of a smoked cigarette [40]. Kubalek et al. (2016) measured uranium, thorium, radium, lead and polonium isotopes in Slovenian cigarettes and estimated that the contribution of thorium isotopes to the effective dose (6, 47 and 37 µSv/year from 228Th, 230Th and 232Th, 90 in total) exceeds that of the 210Po + 210Pb pair (61 + 9 µSv/year) [16]. The review by Felix and Ntarisa of 40 studies gives 232Th 8.1 (0.3–41.0) mBq/g and 238U 15.2 (0.2–82.0) mBq/g in tobacco, and a mean annual dose of 889.7 µSv from 232Th against 295 µSv from 210Po [30]. Papastefanou pointed out that the contribution of radium isotopes is usually disregarded in the literature, although it is of the same order as that of 210Pb [11]. Thus 210Po is the most studied, but not the only alpha emitter in tobacco.

Section 3 Pathways of 210Po and 210Pb uptake by the tobacco plant: root uptake and atmospheric deposition

The pathway by which 210Po and 210Pb enter the tobacco leaf has been debated since the mid-1960s, and both mechanisms were proposed in the first studies. Ermolaeva-Makovskaya et al. (1965) concluded that the 210Po in cigarettes is in equilibrium with 210Pb released on the surface of tobacco leaves, and that 210Pb forms in the air from radon emanating from the soil [4]. Berger, Erhardt and Francis (1965), on the basis of soil-extract analyses, reached a similar conclusion as a hypothesis: there are indications that 210Po or its precursors are taken up not by the roots but by sorption in dead, moist plant material at the atmosphere–plant interface; 210Po was not detected in green tobacco leaf, whereas cured leaf and soils contained it [10]. Tso, Harley and Alexander (1966), in an experiment with a chamber enriched in radon-222, in fields with different phosphate fertilisers and in culture with 210Pb in the nutrient solution, reached the opposite conclusion: the major portion of 210Pb in the plant was probably absorbed through the roots, while airborne radon and its daughters contributed much less; the final 210Po content was affected by the stage of leaf development and the curing method [9]. As reported by Ferri and Baratta, Marsden (1964) considered the contribution of atmospheric polonium fallout minor compared with root uptake [28]; Nikolova (1970), on the contrary, notes that the absence of a correlation between the alpha activity of grass and soil indicates the importance of the foliar pathway [14].

Ferri and Baratta found 210Pb/226Ra activity ratios of 1.9–4.1 in cigarettes (4.3 in pipe tobacco) and concluded that 210Pb reaches tobacco by direct uptake from soil and air rather than merely as the daughter of radium in the tobacco itself [28]. Martell (1974) proposed the mechanism adopted in most subsequent studies: airborne 210Pb is concentrated on small Aitken particles that accumulate on tobacco trichomes; curing and the combustion of trichomes produce insoluble particles of high 210Pb activity that the smoker inhales [13]. Papastefanou (2009) notes that about 85 % of tobacco leaf trichomes have glandular heads coated with sticky exudate and, on the basis of his own measurements, distinguishes the nuclides by origin: 210Pb is deposited from the air and trapped by trichomes; radium isotopes through the roots from soil rather than with fertilisers (in the conclusions of the paper, through the roots or with fertilisers); 40K through the roots; 137Cs and 134Cs through the roots, since four years after the Chernobyl accident they remained in the soil [11]. Persson and Holm (2011) distinguish polonium supported by uptake from soil from unsupported polonium deposited from the atmosphere; for leafy plants the 210Po level is particularly high owing to direct deposition [5]. Berthet et al. adopt the atmospheric mechanism in their introduction [2]; Cankurt and Görgün (2020), who determined 210Po monthly in tobacco from the Aegean region of Turkey, proceed from the capture of aerosols by sticky trichomes on both leaf surfaces; they found in the leaf a mean of 14.1 ± 1.1 Bq/kg dry mass (from 8.3 in June to 21.7 in July), 7.6 in the stem, 14.3 in the root and 34.4 Bq/kg in the soil, a soil-to-leaf transfer factor of 0.22–0.65, and a negative relationship between 210Po in the leaf and rainfall [31]. Ghanbar-Moghaddam and Fathivand (2020) name drying of tobacco in direct contact with fossil-fuel flue gases as an additional source of 210Po [36].

The role of phosphate fertilisers is assessed differently in the sources reviewed. According to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, the specific activity of 226Ra in phosphate fertilisers is approximately 0.185–1.11 Bq/g (5–30 pCi/g) depending on the blend; during processing of phosphate rock about 80 % of the 226Ra concentrates in phosphogypsum [12]. Papastefanou gives 238U 312–936 Bq/kg, 226Ra 19–1129 Bq/kg and 40K 53–6370 Bq/kg for phosphate fertilisers [11]. Zagà et al. (2011) list fertilisers among the main sources with reference to several studies and, citing a 1980 internal industry document, report that Martell’s recommendation to replace calcium phosphate fertilisers with ammonium phosphate was known to manufacturers and assessed by them as costly [8]. The brief review by Zagà, Cattaruzza and Gallus (2024) ranks the pathways by contribution: principal — deposition of radon-222 decay products on the leaf with capture by trichomes; a smaller share — root uptake; additional — fertilisers from raw material rich in uranium [38].

Role of phosphate fertilisers: controlled experiments, industry documents and an input balance

Two controlled experiments have been carried out on tobacco, with opposite conclusions. Tso, Harley and Alexander (1966) measured 13.4 ± 0.6 pCi/g of 226Ra and 6.72 ± 0.14 pCi/g of 210Po (496 and 249 Bq/kg) in a commercial phosphate-containing fertiliser used on tobacco, against 0.95 and 1.05 pCi/g in a mix specially prepared from pure reagents; when a 4-8-12 fertiliser was applied at 1560 kg/ha, an apparent increase in 210Po activity in the soil and in the tobacco was observed, although, in the authors’ assessment, in the large volume of soil treated the amount of fertiliser applied over such a short period did not appear to have a significant effect on the amounts of radioelements available for uptake by plants; from the experiments in a chamber with air enriched in radon-222 and with 210Pb in the nutrient solution, the authors concluded that airborne radon and its decay products contributed much less and that the major portion of 210Pb in the plant was probably absorbed through the roots [9]. Continuing the same series, Tso, Steffens, Ferri and Baratta (1968) found a wide variation of 226Ra, 210Pb and 210Po in soils of tobacco-growing areas and in fertilisers available for tobacco production and concluded that radionuclides from fertiliser applied in one season may not affect the immediate crop but contribute to their build-up in the soil [42]; Tso and Fisenne (1968) showed that 210Pb applied to the leaf is not translocated to other parts of the plant, whereas under root supply both nuclides are distributed among tissues, and that, if rainfall does contribute to the levels of these elements in the leaf, the principal route of entry is probably root absorption [44]; the experiment of Tso, Carr, Ferri and Baratta (1968) on varieties and curing methods is cited from a copy of the text in the industry documents archive [43]. Francis, Chesters and Erhardt (1968), working in Wisconsin like the authors of [10], grew tobacco, corn, ryegrass, snap beans and peas in the greenhouse on soils amended with 100 mg/kg of phosphorus as monoammonium phosphate or as rock phosphate, which by the estimate they cite contains up to 350 mg/kg of uranium (260 pCi/g), and also in the field [41]. In greenhouse tobacco leaf, 210Po was 0.04 ± 0.04 pCi/g with monoammonium phosphate, 0.05 ± 0.04 and 0.04 ± 0.04 pCi/g with rock phosphate (80 and 341 days after harvest) and 0.04 ± 0.04 pCi/g without added phosphate, that is at the detection limit; field tobacco of the same experiment contained 0.02 ± 0.04 pCi/g at harvest and 0.56 ± 0.07 pCi/g after 186 days (ingrowth from 210Pb) [41]; for cured leaf the authors cite 0.34–1.50 pCi/g from Berger et al. [10], [41]. The authors concluded that root uptake of 210Pb from soil as the principal mechanism of 210Po entry into plants was evaluated and found to be invalid, that direct uptake of 210Pb from soil is not a significant mechanism of entry into plants, and that the principal mechanism is deposition of 210Pb with precipitation [41]. The difference between the conclusions of the 1966–1968 series has not been removed by later measurements: the Greek [11], Turkish [31], Polish [65] and summary [5] data were obtained without controlled variation of fertiliser; Skwarzec et al. (2001), from the distribution of 210Po among plant parts, indicated that polonium is generally taken up by tobacco from dry or wet atmospheric deposition [65].

Later measurements concern the soil and the long-lived precursors rather than 210Pb and 210Po of the leaf. Mwalongo et al. (2023), on fields in Tanzania, Kenya and Uganda, found up to 3216 ± 22 Bq/kg of 238U in NPK fertilisers based on local phosphate rock, and significantly more 238U in soil under that fertiliser than in the control (30.6 ± 1.3 Bq/kg); 210Pb and 210Po were not measured in that work, and only 238U, 232Th and 40K were determined in the leaf [45]. According to the IAEA summary, currently exploited sedimentary phosphate ores contain 238U-series radionuclides at about 0.5–3 Bq/g, whereas igneous (apatite) ores contain about 0.1–0.2 Bq/g with an elevated 232Th series (0.1–0.4 Bq/g); application of phosphogypsum with 0.51 Bq/g of 226Ra at 13 and 26 t/ha did not change the 226Ra content of the crops and did not lead to accumulation of activity in the soil, and in a greenhouse experiment with tomatoes at up to 20 t/ha 238U and 226Ra in the crop were below the detection limit and 210Po was 0.4–0.7 Bq/kg dry weight [46]. Lauria et al. (2009) found no differences in radium and uranium between vegetables from organic and conventional farming [48]. Indian experiments with tobacco under different fertilisers (diammonium phosphate, superphosphate, urea, potash, zinc sulphate) showed that the alpha-track density of the leaf depends on the type of fertiliser and increases with time, without separation by nuclide [49], [50]. Boryło, Skwarzec and Wieczorek (2022) name direct deposition of 210Po and 210Pb on the leaf surface as the most important route for large-leaved plants including tobacco, and attribute entry from phosphate fertilisers to tuberous vegetables [47]. In Papastefanou’s paper the wording differs between sections: in the results the radium isotopes are attributed to root uptake from soil rather than from fertilisers, in the conclusions to entry either through the roots or with fertilisers [11].

The internal industry documents relied on by Zagà et al. [8] are available in the Truth Tobacco Industry Documents archive. A Lorillard memorandum of 5 September 1975 sets out Martell’s hypothesis that calcium phosphate fertiliser dust is responsible for the tobacco trichomes sintering into highly insoluble smoke particles, together with his suggestion to switch to ammonium phosphate fertiliser [51]. A Philip Morris memorandum of 2 April 1980 contains the assessment quoted in [8] of the recommendation to use ammonium phosphate instead of calcium phosphate as probably valid but costly (“probably a valid but expensive point”) [52]. U.S. Patent 4,194,514 of 25 March 1980 (Stauffer Chemical) describes the removal of radioactive lead and polonium by treating uncured leaves with a dilute acid solution [53]. The statement by Zagà et al. that American tobacco is 5.5 times more radioactive than Indian tobacco (19.09 versus 3.33 mBq/g) as a result of polyphosphate fertilisers is supported by two references: the measurements of Singh and Nilekani (1976) on Indian tobacco [54] and the chapter by Harley, Cohen and Tso (1978) on 210Po in tobacco [66]; in the same review the first of these also supports another ratio — Indian cigarettes being 6–15 times less radioactive [8]. According to a secondary account, the work of Singh and Nilekani contains measurements of Indian products (0.07–0.10 pCi/g in cigarettes) but neither a comparison with American tobacco nor a conclusion about the cause of the difference [54]; a difference in levels between countries does not by itself point to fertilisers and may be explained, in particular, by climate and by the 210Pb deposition flux.

The order of magnitude is estimated from an input balance. Phosphorus application rates for tobacco in the United States are, according to the Virginia Tech guide, 40 lb of P2O5 per acre (45 kg/ha) on soils with medium and high phosphorus status and up to 230 lb per acre (258 kg/ha, from 500 lb of triple superphosphate) on poor soils [55]; for North Carolina burley, 40–50 lb of P2O5 per acre [56]; in the experiment of Tso et al., 4-8-12 fertiliser was applied at 1560 kg/ha [9]. At 100–560 kg/ha of triple superphosphate and 0.185–1.11 Bq/g of 226Ra [12], one application brings 2–62 Bq/m² of radium; the fertiliser of Tso’s experiment (496 Bq/kg 226Ra, 249 Bq/kg 210Po) at 1560 kg/ha brings 77 Bq/m² of radium and 39 Bq/m² of polonium. The annual atmospheric deposition of 210Pb is 50–500 Bq/m² according to the summary of Boryło et al. [47] and 1–2539 Bq/m² according to the global database of Zhang et al. (2021), with maxima in the mid-latitudes of the Northern Hemisphere [57]. The quantities are of the same order, and the balance does not exclude a contribution from fertilisers. However, fertiliser radium does not pass directly into leaf 210Pb: it does so through the roots at transfer factors of 0.1–0.3 (Section 5) or through exhalation of radon from the soil and deposition of its decay products on the leaf; fertiliser 210Po decays with a half-life of 138 days. The balance therefore gives no grounds to regard fertilisers as the principal source; the direct experiment [41] and the data on herbaceous vegetation (Section 5) point to the atmospheric route as predominant, whereas the Tso series [9], [42], [44] points to the root route. The relative contribution of the pathways is presumably determined by local conditions: the type and rate of fertiliser, the radium content of the soil and the level of deposition in the region.

Section 4 The 210Po/210Pb ratio and storage time of the leaf

The half-life of 210Po (138.4 d) is short compared with that of 210Pb (22.2 years), so after harvest the 210Pb activity is practically constant and the activity ratio relaxes to unity according to R(t) = 1 + (R0 − 1)·e−λt, where R0 is the ratio at harvest and λ is the decay constant of 210Po. For R0 = 0.32 this formula gives 0.72, 0.89 and 0.98 after 180, 365 and 730 d; for R0 = 1.54, 1.22, 1.09 and 1.01.

Measurements on tobacco are consistent with the calculation. Ferri and Baratta observed the disequilibrium directly: in pipe tobacco 210Pb and 210Po were not in equilibrium (0.41 and 0.20 pCi/g, a ratio of 0.49; 210Pb taken equal to the measured 210Bi), which the authors attributed to the tobacco being sold before equilibrium was established, whereas ageing of cigarette tobacco for about two years allows 210Pb and its daughters 210Bi and 210Po to reach equilibrium; in the cigarettes in which all three isotopes were measured (three types of six), their activities agreed within the uncertainty [28]. Khater states that more than two years usually pass between harvest and cigarette production — by his estimate 6–7 half-lives of 210Po (two years correspond to 5.3 half-lives) — and polonium approaches secular equilibrium with lead [7]; Berthet et al. measured a ratio of 1.06 ± 0.05 in cigarettes on the Swiss market [2]. Nikolova, by contrast, explained the lower 210Po content in finished products compared with leaf by decay of polonium during fermentation and warehouse storage [14] — possible only for leaf in which polonium exceeded lead at harvest (R0 > 1), as in Slovenian silage (Section 5).

Consequence for exposure assessment: the 210Po activity in smoked tobacco is determined by the activity of 210Pb accumulated by the leaf during growth, not by the polonium content at harvest. It also follows that 210Pb matters for the smoker’s exposure no less than polonium: it determines both the 210Po activity in the cigarette and — through insoluble smoke particles — the ingrowth of polonium in the tissues themselves (Section 7).

Section 5 Comparison with data for herbaceous vegetation

Tobacco leaf is the above-ground leafy biomass of an annual plant. For such biomass (forage, hay, silage, wild medicinal herbs) a body of 210Pb and 210Po measurements exists that was obtained independently of the smoking problem. The comparison is made on five criteria: specific activity level, 210Po/210Pb ratio, uptake pathway, the role of soil radium, and the strength of binding of deposited lead. The conclusions of this section are the interpretation of the present review, not statements of the cited works.

No accumulation mechanism specific to tobacco is needed to explain this level.

The 210Po/210Pb ratio and time after harvest

In freshly harvested biomass 210Po and 210Pb are not in equilibrium: the activity ratio is 0.32 in Slovenian hay and 1.54 in silage [19]. By the formula of Section 4 both values reach 0.98–1.01 after two years of storage — the same ratio as measured in cigarettes (1.06 ± 0.05 [2]) and predicted by the roughly two-year ageing of cigarette tobacco [7], [28]. The disequilibrium in pipe tobacco (0.49 relative to 210Bi [28]) corresponds by the same formula to storage of about two months at R0 ≈ 0.3, typical of hay.

Uptake pathway

For herbaceous vegetation the sources indicate a predominance of the atmospheric pathway. According to Beckman, the main pathway of vegetation contamination with 210Pb is deposition on above-ground parts with aerosols (about 85 %), the root pathway about 15 %, and the 210Pb content of grass is determined by the amount of precipitation rather than by the soil content [27]. According to Bakhur et al., root uptake accounts for up to 20 % of 210Po and up to 14 % of 210Pb; 210Pb accumulates mainly in the upper, young above-ground parts, and the roots of bilberry and marsh tea contain 2–3 times less polonium and 3–4 times less lead than the above-ground part [26]. Pietrzak-Fliś and Skowrońska-Smolak (1995), in an experiment comparing an open field with a polyethylene tent, found that in the above-ground parts of plants on the open field the 210Pb and 210Po activities were higher than under the tent, whereas in the roots they were nearly the same; the authors regard transfer via the root system as negligible and name atmospheric, mainly wet, deposition as the main source [23]; according to Persson’s compilation (2013), the deposition transfer factor for grass and leafy plants is 0.5–1 (Bq/kg plant per Bq/m² deposited), 0.1–0.5 for less leafy plants and straw, below 0.2 and below 0.003 for grain and root crops, and about three times higher for 210Po than for 210Pb [24]. Jovanovič (2011), on the tailings of the former Žirovski Vrh uranium mine, found that the measured 210Pb activity in grass was up to a hundred times higher than that calculated from the soil content, and attributed this to deposition of radon-222 decay products from the air [22].

Tobacco leaf is broad and densely covered with glandular trichomes [11], so by its surface properties it presumably captures airborne particles no less efficiently than herbaceous vegetation. By analogy, the data for herbaceous vegetation support the atmospheric mechanism [2], [4], [5], [10], [13], [41] and are inconsistent with the conclusion of Tso et al. [9]. The analogy does not, however, refute the result of a controlled experiment: no paired open-field versus shelter measurements on tobacco were found in the sources reviewed, and the two controlled experiments on tobacco (Section 3) gave opposite results.

Soil radium and fertilisers

According to the IAEA handbook (Technical Reports Series No. 472), the mean soil-to-plant transfer factor for stems and shoots of grasses (category Grasses) in temperate climates is 0.31 for lead (N = 17) and 0.13 for radium (N = 62), and for pasture vegetation 0.092 for lead, 0.12 for polonium (N = 10) and 0.071 for radium [25]. The soil-to-leaf factor for 210Po in tobacco, 0.22–0.65 [31], is of the same order. With such values root uptake from soil radium is limited, and raising the soil radium content through fertilisers presumably affects 210Pb and 210Po in the leaf mainly through radon emanation from the soil and deposition of its decay products on the leaf. The tailings site [22] represents the limiting case of this mechanism: the radium of the substrate affects 210Pb in grass through the air, not through the root.

In paired measurements of forage and hay the 210Pb/226Ra ratio is 3.2 [18], 11.5 and 24 [19] (per [24]), whereas in the soil under the Brazilian forage 210Pb and 226Ra are close (104 and 135 Bq/kg [24]), i.e. 210Pb in above-ground biomass greatly exceeds the level supported by the radium of the sample itself. In cigarettes this ratio is 1.9–4.1, in pipe tobacco 4.3 [28], and in Greek tobacco leaf before production 4.2 on average (14.1 and 3.4 Bq/kg) [11]: the same sign of disequilibrium and the same order as in forage. It follows that 210Pb in tobacco cannot be estimated from the 226Ra content of the leaf or soil; this is consistent with Papastefanou’s conclusion that radium in tobacco leaf is of root origin [11].

Strength of binding of deposited lead

According to Persson’s compilation (2013), after thorough washing 82 ± 20 % of atmospherically deposited 210Pb and 60 ± 20 % of root-derived 210Pb remain in the plant [24]. Tobacco data are consistent with this: according to Philip Morris internal documents of the mid-1970s, washing the leaf with non-polar solvents reduced its radioactivity by 10–40 %, and according to the testimony of a former company scientist about half of the activity was removed, the remainder being within the leaf tissue [15]. Lead deposited on the leaf does not constitute easily removable surface contamination either in grass or in tobacco.

Volatility and disequilibrium in smoke

Berthet et al., heating tobacco uniformly over 50–600 °C, established that half of the 210Po is lost at 255.9 ± 2.2 °C, whereas for 210Pb the same fraction is lost at 531 ± 17 °C; at 300 °C about 80 % of the polonium is released [2]. The difference in volatility, in the authors’ assessment, may partly explain the different transfers to mainstream smoke (13.6 % for 210Po and 7 % for 210Pb) and the disequilibrium of the isotopes in smoke: the lead-supported fraction of polonium in mainstream smoke averaged 52 ± 16 % with a spread of 38–83 % between runs [2]. Ermolaeva-Makovskaya et al., extracting filters with ether, established that 210Po is practically not associated with the products of dry distillation of tobacco [4]; Kelley related it to the mass of particulate matter [34]; Martell and Radford — to insoluble particles from sintered trichome tips, enriched in 210Pb, from which polonium is released on combustion [13], [32].

Heated tobacco products

For heated tobacco (IQOS Heets, a separate product) Berthet et al. obtained a mainstream transfer of 1.8 ± 0.3 % of 210Po and about 1.5 % of 210Pb, with a 210Po content of 23.7 ± 2.1 mBq/g in the stick — the same as in cigarettes; 78 % of the activity remained in the unconsumed tobacco [2]. The difference is due to incomplete heating of the tobacco: only about 15 % of the tobacco in the stick reaches the target temperature of 330 °C, whereas uniform heating to 300 °C would release about 80 % of the 210Po [2], [6], [38]. The authors point out that a lower dose is not equivalent to a lower risk, since the calculation does not account for the consumption pattern or its possible increase [6].

Section 7 Behaviour of 210Po and 210Pb in the airways and tissues

The cigarette smoke aerosol has a particle diameter of about 300 nm [17]; according to measurements by Desorgher et al., 92 % of mainstream 210Po is retained by a 0.8 µm filter, and the median of the size distribution adopted in their model is 0.43 µm [6]. In both radon-exposed miners and smokers, 75 % of lung tumours are found in the branching airways of the bronchial tree and 15 % in the gas exchange region [17]. Deposition in the bronchial region, according to the calculation of Desorgher et al., is about 13 % of the inhaled activity — substantially more than in the standard ICRP models [6].

Little, Radford, McCombs and Hunt (1965) measured 210Po in the lung tissues of smokers [33]; according to later accounts, this and a subsequent study by the same group (1967) found the deposit to be non-uniform, with hot spots at bronchial bifurcations [6], where 210Po is thought to deposit and bronchogenic carcinomas frequently arise [15]. Desorgher et al. note that these measurements remain the only data on the localisation of 210Po in the bronchi of deceased smokers, and that in their absence dosimetry relies on general models [6]. Martell (1974) and Radford and Martell (1975) linked local accumulation to insoluble particles enriched in 210Pb: polonium is released from them on combustion and grows in again after inhalation, so, on the assumption that all polonium arises from ingrowth in the particles, the 210Po/210Pb ratio allows their residence time in tissue to be estimated; preliminary data (three smokers, two non-smokers) gave 3–5 months for smokers, an estimate the authors consider likely to be low [13], [32].

In Ferri and Baratta, 210Po in the lung parenchyma of smokers is three times that of the non-smoker, 2.8 times in the pulmonary lymph nodes and 2.4 times in the liver, with small samples indicating, in the authors’ assessment, only a trend [28]. Section 8 Radiation dose to the smoker

Dose estimates in the literature refer to three different quantities: the local tissue dose at deposition sites, the mean lung dose and the effective dose. These quantities differ by orders of magnitude and are not directly comparable.

Local doses to the bronchial epithelium

The first estimate belongs to Radford and Hunt: according to the abstract, for a person smoking two packs a day the dose to the bronchial epithelium is probably at least seven times that from background sources, and in localised areas may reach 1000 rem or more in 25 years [1]. The abstract gives no value for the averaged dose, and secondary sources differ: Nikolova gives about 36 rem in 25 years [14], Petushkov et al. 200 rem in 25 years [40]; the latter value coincides with the estimate of Little and Radford (1967) for the epithelium of lower-lobe bifurcations — 2 Sv over 25 years of smoking, cited by Zagà et al. [8]. Other estimates of the same class collected by Nikolova and Zagà: 1 rem in 25 years according to Rajewsky and 1 rem per year according to Hill (1965) [14]; 80 mSv/year to the epithelium at bifurcations for one and a half packs a day according to Winters and DiFranza (1982) [11]; about 16 Sv (80 rad) of cumulative alpha dose at the bifurcations of smokers who died of lung cancer according to Martell [8]. Zagà et al. (2011) cite from Radford and Hunt only the maximum value — up to 0.4 Sv a year or 10 Sv over 25 years — without indicating its local character [8].

Mean lung dose

Ferri and Baratta calculated the mean dose to the lungs as a whole (mass 1200 g, all inhaled material retained, effective half-time 18 d) for two packs a day: 33–92 mrem/year (0.33–0.92 mSv/year) depending on the brand; for the brand containing pipe tobacco the paper’s table gives 92 and the text 95 mrem/year [28]. The authors state that, allowing for the fraction of aerosol deposited in and cleared from the lungs, the values could be high by as much as a factor of eight, and that non-homogeneous deposition may produce local doses much greater than the mean [28].

The data of Table 6 show that the more than tenfold spread of effective doses (0.06–0.8 mSv/year) is due mainly to three calculation assumptions rather than to the activity of the tobacco, which is of the same order for most authors (10–38 mBq per cigarette). The first is the inhaled fraction: Khater assumed 74.7 % in smoke and 50 % inhalation, i.e. 0.37 of the cigarette activity; Papastefanou 0.75; Desorgher et al. the measured 0.136 for polonium and 0.07 for lead. Desorgher et al. note that some authors use a transfer factor of 0.7–1.0, whereas measurements give less than 0.15, and that estimates in the literature range from 30 µSv to tens of mSv per year [6]; the upper part of this range lies outside Table 6. The second is the dose coefficient: for 210Pb it differs by a factor of about 27 (1.1 µSv/Bq in Papastefanou against 29.2 in Desorgher), depending on the absorption type assumed and on whether the enhanced deposition in the bronchial region is taken into account. The third is the absorption regime: moving from slow to moderate absorption, one and the same model gives 0.317 and 0.072 mSv/year [6]. Laking’s systematic review (2019) reaches a similar conclusion: the results remain sensitive to pharmacological assumptions about absorption and redistribution of inhaled radionuclides and to radiobiological assumptions, and a subset of contemporary authors still suggests individual health risks about 1000 times higher than internationally accepted models support [29].

The UNSCEAR 2000 report estimates the annual effective dose from uranium- and thorium-series radionuclides in body tissues at 120 µSv, the main contributor being 210Po; the value refers to the general population, no smoker-specific dose estimate is given in Volume I, but it is noted that tobacco smoking substantially increases the intake of 210Pb and 210Po by smokers [17]. The same report states that the combined effect of smoking and radon on lung cancer risk is the only case for which epidemiological data clearly call for allowance for a more-than-additive effect [17].

A local dose of 10 Sv over 25 years (400 mSv/year) exceeds the effective doses of Table 6 for conventional cigarettes by a factor of 500–6800; this compares unlike quantities — a tissue dose at the deposition site and a dose averaged over organs with weighting factors. Ferri and Baratta stated this distinction explicitly in 1966: their calculation refers to the dose averaged over the entire lung tissue, whereas non-homogeneous deposition gives substantially higher local doses [28].

Estimates of risk and of the share of lung cancer cases

Attribution of “1–2 % of lung cancer cases”

The statement that 210Po is responsible for 2 % of smoking-induced lung cancer cases appears in popular accounts without reference to a specific study. The nearest published estimate of this kind is Radford’s letter to the New England Journal of Medicine (1982), according to which 210Po is responsible for about 1 % of lung cancers in the United States; Muggli et al., citing this estimate, convert it into more than 1600 deaths a year in the United States and 11,700 worldwide [15]. No peer-reviewed study substantiating the 2 % figure by calculation was found in preparing this review. Zagà et al. estimate the risk differently — 4 × 10−4 cases per year per smoker, i.e. 4 cases per 10 000 smokers per year, noting that the estimate does not account for the co-carcinogenic action [8]. The comparison with “300 chest X-rays a year” for one and a half packs a day, going back to Winters and DiFranza (1982) [15], depends on the dose assumed per radiograph (0.034 mSv in Zagà et al. [8]) and is not reproducible without that basis being stated.

Section 9 Contribution of 210Po and 210Pb to the smoker’s risk: comparison with other causes of death and with indoor radon

The estimates of Section 8 refer to dose. To compare the radiation exposure with other components of the harm from smoking, a transition to risk is made by three independent methods: by the published attribution of a share of lung cancer cases, by effective dose with a nominal risk coefficient, and by local dose. The methods give different values, and the discrepancy between them characterises the uncertainty of the estimate.

Share in smoking-related mortality

According to the 2014 report of the U.S. Surgeon General, active smoking causes 437,400 deaths a year from disease in the United States among adults aged 35 and over, and 439,033 including perinatal deaths and deaths in residential fires (sum over the rows of the table; 2005–2009): lung cancer 127,700 (29.1 %; see below), other malignant neoplasms 36,000, coronary heart disease 99,300, other heart disease 25,500, cerebrovascular disease 15,300, other vascular disease 11,500, diabetes mellitus 9000, pneumonia, influenza and tuberculosis 12,500, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease 100,600, perinatal conditions 1013, residential fires 620; a further 41,280 deaths are due to secondhand smoke [58]. In the lung cancer row the total column of the table gives 130,659; the value 127,700 is obtained as the difference between the subtotal for malignant neoplasms (163,700) and other neoplasms (36,000) and is consistent with the sum of the rows; it is adopted here. Radford’s 1982 estimate that 210Po is responsible for about 1 % of lung cancer cases in the United States (Section 8) gives about 1300 deaths a year, that is 0.3 % of deaths from active smoking; the figure of 1600 given by Muggli et al. on the basis of 162,460 lung cancer deaths [15] corresponds to 0.36 %. The estimate of Zagà et al., 4 × 10−4 cases a year per smoker [8], was derived from the local dose at bifurcations rather than from the effective dose; its conversion to lifetime risk is considered in the next subsection.

Lifetime risk from effective dose

According to the 2007 ICRP Recommendations, the combined detriment from radiation-induced cancer and heritable effects is around 5 % per Sv [59]; below, the nominal coefficient for cancer in the whole population, 5.5 × 10−2 Sv−1, is used (Table 1 of the Recommendations; the table is not reproduced in the freely available extract [59]). At an effective dose of 0.06–0.8 mSv/year (Table 6) over 50 years of smoking, the lifetime risk is 0.017–0.22 % (0.083 % at 0.3 mSv/year). The cumulative risk of lung cancer by age 75 for a smoker at zero radon concentration in the pooled European analysis of Darby et al. is 10.1 % [60], and about half of persistent smokers die of smoking-related causes according to the 50-year follow-up of British doctors [61]; relative to these values, 0.017–0.22 % amounts to 0.2–2 % of a smoker’s lung cancer and 0.03–0.4 % of all deaths from smoking. The central dose-based estimate (0.08 %) and Radford’s attribution (1 % of lung cancer, that is 0.1 % lifetime) agree in order of magnitude; the estimate of Zagà (4 × 10−4 a year, over 50 years 2 % lifetime, i.e. about 20 % of a smoker’s lung cancer and 4 % of all deaths from smoking) is 20 times higher. The ICRP coefficient is intended for protection purposes rather than for calculating individual risk and does not reflect the local alpha dose at bifurcations; the interval of estimates of the contribution of 210Po and 210Pb to smokers’ mortality should therefore be taken as wide — from 0.03 to 4 % of all deaths from smoking, with a central value of the order of 0.2–0.3 % — and none of the estimates attributes the main part of the harm from smoking to radiation.

Interaction with indoor radon exposure: common target tissue, aerosol modification and synergism

Exposure to indoor radon and to polonium in smoke is related in three respects. First, a common target and mechanism of action: Martell (1983) calculated the alpha dose to the epithelium of a smoker’s bronchial bifurcations as the sum of contributions from indoor radon progeny and from 210Po in smoke [62]. Second, modification of the radon progeny aerosol by smoke: in the measurements of Reineking and Porstendörfer (1990), in closed rooms without additional aerosol sources the unattached fraction of the potential alpha energy averaged 0.096 at an equilibrium factor of 0.30, whereas with cigarette smoke and other aerosol sources it fell below the detection limit of 0.005 with equilibrium factors up to 0.77 [63]; the dose coefficient per unit exposure changes accordingly, and estimates that depend on aerosol parameters differ for smokers and non-smokers. Third, synergism in epidemiological data: according to Darby et al., the proportionate increase in risk per 100 Bq/m³ (16 %) is the same in smokers and non-smokers, but the absolute risk of lung cancer death by age 75 rises at 0, 100 and 400 Bq/m³ from 0.41 to 0.47 and 0.67 % in non-smokers and from 10.1 to 11.6 and 16.0 % in smokers [60]. UNSCEAR states that, with the exception of radiation and smoking, epidemiological data give little indication of a need to adjust for strong synergistic or antagonistic combined effects, and that for radon and smoking the interaction leads to more-than-additive effects [17].

Consequences for risk estimates. The effective dose from radon at the worldwide average concentration of 40 Bq/m³ (equilibrium factor 0.4; 7000 h a year; 9 nSv per Bq·h·m−3 [64]) is about 1.0 mSv/year — of the same order as or larger than the 0.06–0.8 mSv/year from cigarettes; at 100 Bq/m³ it is 2.5 mSv/year. The increase in a smoker’s absolute risk from 100 Bq/m³, 1.5 percentage points (from 10.1 to 11.6 %) [60], is about 15 times the central estimate of the contribution of 210Po and 210Pb (0.1 percentage point). In addition, the risk from 210Po and 210Pb is part of the smoker’s baseline risk (10.1 % at 0 Bq/m³), and the increase due to radon is superimposed on it; models that attribute a smoker’s lung cancer to the alpha dose at bifurcations treat radon and polonium together [62], and adding an estimate of that kind to an epidemiological estimate for radon results in double counting.

Section 10 The tobacco industry’s position

Criticism of Radford and Hunt’s original estimates in the peer-reviewed literature consists mainly of methodological work on the transfer of the isotope to smoke (Section 6) and on dosimetric assumptions (Section 8), rather than of a refutation of the hypothesis of its contribution to exposure. Muggli et al. (2008), using about 1500 internal industry documents, showed that manufacturers learned of the presence of polonium in tobacco more than 40 years before publication, confirmed the published levels with their own measurements, attempted to remove it — by washing the leaf (10–40 %), selecting raw material, filters and genetic engineering of the plant — without success; Philip Morris’s 210Po monitoring laboratory, operating from 1981–1982, was, according to the testimony of a former company scientist, closed around 1986 to avoid accumulating data that could be used against the company in litigation [15]. The article’s title (Waking a Sleeping Giant) reproduces the wording of a 1978 internal note whose author questioned the advisability of publishing the company’s own results [8], [15]. The authors propose a radiation-exposure warning on cigarette packs [15].

Section 12 Conclusion

The presence of 210Po and 210Pb in tobacco and the transfer of part of their activity to smoke have been confirmed by independent measurements over sixty years in different countries. The mean specific activities of 210Po in cigarette tobacco obtained in the 1960s (12–20 Bq/kg) and in the 2000s–2020s (13–25 Bq/kg) lie in the same interval; higher values, up to 40–60 Bq/kg, refer to unprocessed raw tobacco and to particular markets, and no consistent long-term trend has been established across studies. This interval lies within the range measured for herbaceous biomass; the 210Pb/226Ra ratio in tobacco, typically 2–4, matches that of forage in sign and order and shows that lead enters the leaf independently of soil radium; the 210Po/210Pb ratio, close to unity in cigarettes and below unity in pipe tobacco, is consistent with ingrowth of polonium during storage of the leaf. Alongside polonium, tobacco contains 228Th, 226Ra and 228Ra at activities of the same order, whose dose contribution is rarely taken into account.

The pathway of isotope uptake into the leaf remains a matter of disagreement between sources: controlled experiments on tobacco gave opposite results — the series of Tso et al. (1966–1968) pointed to root uptake, the greenhouse experiment of Francis et al. (1968) to atmospheric input; measurements on tobacco and on herbaceous vegetation, where atmospheric uptake of 210Pb predominates even on a substrate with high radium content, are consistent with the mechanism of deposition of radon decay products on trichomes. Mainstream smoke carries 3–36 % of the 210Po activity depending on the brand and smoking regime; values around 50 % refer to the sum of mainstream and sidestream, up to 75 % to everything not left in the ash and filter. Polonium is more volatile than lead, so the isotopes are not in equilibrium in smoke, and in the bronchi polonium grows in again from insoluble particles enriched in 210Pb.

Effective doses from 210Po and 210Pb for a pack-a-day smoker calculated with explicit assumptions, 0.06–0.8 mSv/year, differ mainly in the assumed inhaled fraction (from the measured 0.07–0.14 to the assumed 0.75, up to 1.0 for some authors) and in dose coefficients, not in the activity of the tobacco; the local dose to the epithelium of bronchial bifurcations is a quantity of different nature, and a direct comparison of the two is incorrect. The estimate of “2 % of lung cancer cases” is not supported by a peer-reviewed calculation; the nearest published estimate is about 1 %, from Radford’s 1982 letter.

The contribution of phosphate fertilisers has not been established: the series of experiments by Tso et al. in 1966–1968 pointed to the root pathway and to accumulation of fertiliser radionuclides in the soil, the greenhouse experiment of Francis et al. in 1968 found no uptake from phosphate-amended soil, and the balance of radium applied with fertiliser against the annual deposition of 210Pb gives quantities of the same order at the rates used for tobacco. By three independent ways of estimation, the contribution of 210Po and 210Pb to smoking-related mortality is from 0.03 to 4 %, with a central value of the order of 0.2–0.3 % (about 1 % of lung cancer cases); the increase in a smoker’s risk from indoor radon at 100 Bq/m³ is about 15 times this central estimate, and estimates based on the local dose at bifurcations treat radon and polonium together and are not additive with epidemiological estimates for radon.
